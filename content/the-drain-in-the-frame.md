# The Drain in the Frame
*What a photograph can tell us about waste in Lagos*

By Yaqub Tawab Olamilekan

In [Beyond the Piles of Refuse][first-article], I wrote about passing through Obalende on my way to and from work. I could smell the rubbish in the morning and smell it again on my way home. What I couldn’t tell was whether it had been there all day or whether someone had cleared it and more had piled up.

The photograph of Obalende Canal by Eniola Daniel, which I shared in that piece, shows what was there on 23 November. It made me wonder what I’d learn if I had a second photograph taken from the same spot a week later. Would I be able to tell what had changed? And could a computer help me compare the two?

> **Photo · Obalende Canal, 23 November**

I thought back to Mile 12 Ilé-Ilé, where I’d seen rubbish disappear from one side of the road and turn up on the other. If I’d pointed my camera at the original spot both times, I might have thought the problem had been sorted. The first decision in that comparison would have been mine. Where should I point the camera?

The waste series will continue with a map of dated observations across Lagos. Here I want to look more closely at what goes into one of those observations. Before I put a point on a map, I want to know what I can reliably tell from the photograph behind it.

## What would I ask a computer to look for?

I’d start with one question. Can a computer mark the part of a drain where rubbish is visible?

I would trace the edges of the drain in each photo, then mark the patches where I can make out discarded bags, bottles, or other refuse. I could compare those markings with the areas a computer vision model identifies. The result would be an estimate of how much of the visible drain is covered in the picture. I’d still need other measurements to say how much waste was actually there.

A bag caught on a wall is easy to point to. A dark patch near the water could be wet paper or a shadow. I’d mark patches like that as uncertain. I’d also try lining up fixed points in the photographs, such as a corner of the wall, before comparing them. A pile can take up more of the frame simply because I stood closer the second time.

## The photograph I would normally skip

At Ojuelegba, I noticed the LAWMA container because rubbish had collected around it. That’s when I would think to take a picture. If I passed after the area had been cleared, I might put my phone away and keep walking.

For a small experiment, I’d need pictures from both kinds of days. I’d start at a few familiar spots, take dated photographs from roughly the same position, and keep some taken in different light. Then I’d put my markings beside the model’s and look at where they differ. Does a shadow get marked as waste? Does a clear plastic bag get missed when it’s wet? Those are the examples I’d want to understand before trying the same method on more streets.

## What a photograph leaves out

Two photos could show that a pile was present on Monday and gone on Wednesday. A trader who opens there every morning might be able to tell me when a truck came or whether someone moved the bags across the road. I’d want to ask before writing that the spot was cleared by a collection crew.

That’s the question I want to explore in Artificial Intelligence for Image Processing and Computer Vision (IPCVAI). How can I make a fair comparison between images taken under everyday conditions, and how can I tell when the images leave too much uncertainty? I can begin with a stretch of road I know, two dated photographs, and the things I’d still need to ask someone who was there.
